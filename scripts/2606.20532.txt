Title: How Do Instructions Shape Speech? Cross-Attention Attribution for Style-Captioned Text-to-Speech. This paper is essential for understanding how individual words in style captions influence synthesized speech, which is key for improving text-to-speech or TTS system control.

The core contribution of this study is in its novel application of cross-attention attribution to style-captioned TTS systems for the first time. It adapts the Diffusion Attentive Attribution Maps framework to the speech domain, specifically examining the CapSpeech system. The research sheds light on the mechanics of how style instructions affect voice characteristics in a generative model, addressing a fundamental gap in the interpretability of expressive speech synthesis.

Here are the highlights: the study analyzed 3,600 combinations of style captions and text transcripts, revealing that style tokens—essentially the adjectives describing voice qualities like "calm" or "loud"—shape audio waveforms quite distinctly compared to content and function tokens. Style tokens showed a notably lower temporal variance, meaning they influence the output uniformly across time, confirming global conditioning. They also correlated strongly with acoustic parameters like pitch and energy. For instance, tokens like "loud" showed a strong correlation with energy, indicating high attention where the speech is loudest. Interestingly, style conditioning hit its peak in the early steps of the generation process and deep layers of the model, with maximum network selectivity occurring at a stage when style is most critical. This was indicated by low attention entropy, suggesting the system focuses more precisely on relevant tokens. Another fascinating insight was that style tokens exhibit a distinctive pattern of having low variance yet high peak-to-mean ratio, implying that these tokens create a consistent and characteristic attention map stretched across the timeline.

One notable limitation of this analysis is its scope, which was limited to the CapSpeech model with synthetic prompts. Expanding to other models and naturally occurring prompts would be beneficial.

In conclusion, this paper will be particularly insightful for researchers and practitioners interested in the interpretability of TTS systems. It underscores the importance of understanding global conditioning effects in speech synthesis, which could drive advancements in creating more expressive and controllable TTS systems.
