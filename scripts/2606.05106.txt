Arithmetic Pedagogy for Language Models is a paper that could redefine how we teach machines to calculate, using human learning methods as a guide. The core of this study investigates whether we can guide the training of small language models to perform arithmetic by utilizing human teaching strategies, specifically the Indonesian GASING method. It uniquely operationalizes arithmetic operations as computational procedures, serialized into natural-language Chain-of-Thought supervision for training a small-scale language model without any fancy reinforcements or reward tricks.

Here's what stands out: they trained a variant of the GPT-2 language model, specifically a smaller version with only 86 million parameters, using an Indonesian-specific tokenizer. This model achieved over 80% accuracy on arithmetic tasks that it hadn't seen before. Remarkably, it matched up well against much larger models, showing that size isn't everything when you've got the right training strategy. The approach employs a step-by-step left-to-right solving method, reflecting how humans might naturally do mental arithmetic. This is counterintuitive because it departs from the usual right-to-left computational approaches, especially for operations like addition.

What's fascinating is how the model develops: it starts by learning the basic syntax and language structure, and then, as training progresses, it moves into internally correcting its computations. This is akin to humans developing the ability to perform "mental arithmetic" without needing to compute every step explicitly. They also uncovered three distinct learning phases, signifying the stages of arithmetic proficiency the model goes through.

A notable limitation is the focus on Indonesian numerals and language — this means the approach may not directly transpose to other languages or numeral systems without adaptation.

In the end, this study is essential for those interested in machine learning and computational education. It shows that with the right pedagogical approach, even small models can outperform expectations, potentially providing a more efficient way to enhance machine cognitive abilities in arithmetic without colossal resources.
