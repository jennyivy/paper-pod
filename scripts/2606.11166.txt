The paper "Flaws in the LLM Automation Narrative," presents a timely critique of the overestimated abilities of Large Language Models, or LLMs, suggesting they're not as reliable as advertised for high-stakes tasks, particularly in comparison to human experts.

At the heart of the paper, the authors challenge the assertion that LLMs can replace human experts in the knowledge economy. By designing a new benchmarking task that requires writing computer code for data analysis, they compared a leading LLM against human experts. The core finding was that human experts consistently outperformed the LLM on a range of metrics and exhibited less variability in their performance.

Here are the highlights. When tested on a task from the 2016 American Causal Inference Conference involving data analysis with 7,700 datasets, the LLM exhibited significant performance issues. Out of 20 scripts generated by the LLM for this task, three scripts failed to run. Furthermore, the first two LLM-generated scripts displayed errors with astounding magnitudes, producing errors over 100 billion standard deviations larger than the outcome variable. For context, something over 0.8 standard deviations is normally considered a large effect size. This shows LLMs may yield catastrophic failures.

When it came to measuring bias, the pattern was similar. Many of the LLM submissions showed bias thousands of times worse than submissions from human experts, with two exceeding a billion standard deviations. Even the best-performing scripts from the LLM, which implemented causal forests, failed to consistently outperform human experts.

Evaluations on the magnitude and distribution of errors also indicated that LLM results were unreliable. Human experts had minor variance in their results, while the LLM produced wildly inconsistent outputs. Standard deviation for Root Mean Squared Error for human submissions was a fraction of a unit, whereas for the LLM it spanned into the billions. This inconsistency shows that, while the LLM can occasionally hit human-level performance, it's unreliable and can't be trusted for consistent quality.

One important caveat to note is that improving performance through better prompts or prompt engineering might require substantial domain expertise. But this adjustment wouldn't reflect the real capability of LLMs, but rather the expertise of the human directing it.

The takeaway here is clear. Current LLMs aren't ready to replace human experts, especially where consistency and reliability are crucial. Developers and practitioners should treat claims of LLMs matching expert human intelligence with skepticism. For those working in fields like data analysis or anywhere high-stakes decisions rely on nuanced judgment, this paper suggests that human oversight remains indispensable.
