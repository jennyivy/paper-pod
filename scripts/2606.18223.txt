Learning Red Agent Policy from Observations for Neurosymbolic Autonomous Cyber Agents is a cutting-edge paper that addresses pressing challenges in cybersecurity with sophisticated autonomous systems.

This work tackles the difficult problem of predicting a cyber-attacker’s actions in a network environment where the attacker, referred to as the red agent, operates covertly. The authors propose a novel policy learning technique using imitation learning to predict these elusive red actions from observable network states and defender or blue actions. The core of this contribution lies in marrying neurosymbolic approaches—combining the interpretability of behavior trees with the adaptiveness of reinforcement learning components—to bolster autonomous network defenses against cyber threats.

Among the notable results of this research is its implementation within the CybORG CAGE Challenge 2 environment, a realistic simulation that mirrors real-world cyber scenarios using tactics from the MITRE ATT&CK knowledge base. The proposed policy learning technique achieves high prediction accuracies across diverse strategic scenarios, boasting low prediction error margins under different adversarial strategies, even when dynamic strategy switching is involved. For instance, under the B-Line red agent strategy, which is deterministic and direct, their method excels in prediction accuracy, showcasing its capability to anticipate and adapt to red actions with precision. It performs robustly, managing prediction errors effectively, even when facing the meandering strategy, which is more random and exploratory in nature.

One particularly intriguing aspect of their results is the system's ability to generalize learned policies to untrained scenarios, exhibiting notable adaptability and the potential for real-world applications in dynamic environments. Their approach also enables the estimation of network intrusion levels in real-time, allowing defenders not only to predict but also to assess the effects of these predictions on overall network security. However, a key limitation highlighted is the dependency on a simulated environment, which may not perfectly capture the complexities of all real-world networks. Future work will need to focus on validating this approach in more realistic, perhaps emulated settings to ensure its efficacy outside of controlled simulations.

This paper has significant implications for those involved in cyber defense, particularly those developing solutions for dynamic and partially observable environments. Practitioners are encouraged to explore this blend of neurosymbolic methodologies to enhance predictive capabilities and strengthen cybersecurity measures in real-time, as it offers an advanced frontier for precise and adaptive network protection strategies.
