Introducing HAMON: Passive Optical Sequence Mixing for Long-Horizon Forecasting, a groundbreaking approach in the field of long-term time series prediction. This paper is significant because it explores the potential of using optical rather than digital methods for forecasting, challenging the conventional digital frameworks.

HAMON's core contribution is reimagining time series forecasting through a passive optical system. It uses a diffractive optical core where historical data is encoded into an optical field. The forecast is made by a single pass of light through trainable phase masks, rather than relying on computationally intensive digital layers. This approach aligns with the growing evidence that many forecasting tasks can be effectively handled by simpler, linear models, thus leveraging the inherent linearity of optical processes.

What stands out in HAMON is its performance on standard benchmarks, where it surpasses digital baselines on specific datasets like ETTm2, improving mean squared error by up to 14 percent consistently across various horizons. HAMON competes well on the Weather dataset and performs reasonably on others, though it lags behind digital methods in datasets requiring high-channel count like Traffic and Electricity. The implementation isn't reliant on digital forecasting heads, confirmed by ablations showing that optical signals drive the forecasts. This deviation from typical digital models is both surprising and elegant.

A critical limitation to note is that HAMON, while innovative, isn’t a fully optical solution. It retains some digital elements at the boundaries like normalization and input encoding. However, the sequence-mixing core itself is entirely passive and optical.

In conclusion, HAMON opens up a new avenue for researchers interested in physically accelerated forecasting. It’s a fascinating concept for anyone looking into hardware-aware machine learning models and suggests that there might be more efficient, material-based solutions in areas traditionally dominated by digital computation.
