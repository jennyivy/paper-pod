The paper "FACTR 2: Learning External Force Sensing for Commodity Robot Arms Improves Policy Learning" tackles a significant challenge in robotics—enabling low-cost robot arms to sense force without expensive sensors. This advancement is pivotal because it broadens access to force-sensitive capabilities, previously restricted to high-end robotic systems.

The core contribution of this work lies in its new method called Neural External Torque Estimation, or NEXT, which estimates external joint torque on robot arms without needing dedicated force-torque sensors. NEXT can be trained with just ten minutes of data and one minute of processing, yet it achieves accuracy comparable to high-end torque sensors. This capability is complemented by Force-Informed Re-Sampling Training, or FIRST, which enhances policy learning by focusing on critical phases of robotic tasks using the torque information provided by NEXT.

This study brings several highlights to the table. NEXT enables accurate force estimation on commodity and low-cost robot arms like the Piper, which previously could not leverage precise force feedback due to lack of sensors. Impressively, NEXT achieves an average error of point zero one eight Newton-meters on these arms, outperforming traditional methods. Moreover, in trials on more expensive systems like the Franka robot arm, NEXT matched the performance of dedicated joint torque sensors, achieving an error reduction of over sixty percent compared to older methods. FIRST, an integral part of this system, uses these estimations to markedly improve task performance. On five complicated, contact-heavy tasks, FIRST boosted task success rates by an average of seventeen percent. It was particularly adept at navigating pre-contact and contact phases, areas where typical robotic policies often falter.

The main limitation of NEXT is its dependency on accurate robot specifics, like the motor torque constant, which can affect the absolute torque scale. Calibration might be needed if these parameters are off, though this seldom impacts teleoperation comfort or policy learning due to normalization practices.

In essence, FACTR 2 is a game-changer for anyone working with robotic arms, especially those constrained by budget. It democratizes access to force-sensitive techniques in robotics, allowing these systems to perform more complex tasks with greater accuracy and reliability. Check it out if you aim to enhance robotic manipulation or teleoperation affordably.
