Today's paper is "The Role of Feedback Alignment in Self-Distillation" by Semih Kara and Oğuzhan Ersoy. This research could reshape how we think about improving machine learning models through self-distillation by focusing on the feedback's structure.

The authors tackle the challenge of refining self-distillation processes by using feedback alignment. They found a novel method that involves tailoring feedback to align with a model's errors and reasoning. This approach redefines how language models distill knowledge from context-rich situations and apply it later in context-free settings.

Let's jump into what they achieved. They tested three feedback conditions: binary rewards, reference solutions, and step-aligned critique. Remarkably, the step-aligned method improved performance by over 16 points compared to binary feedback, and more than 5 points over the reference solution method, according to the Average at 12 test. Why does this work so well? Because it targets the precise moments in a reasoning trace where the model errs, preserving correct actions or tokens and only adjusting where necessary. By comparison, reference solutions often lead to unnecessary changes across the board due to differences in surface form, even when the underlying reasoning is correct.

Here's something surprising: the study showed that the structural alignment of feedback is just as crucial as the quality or correctness of that feedback. A perfect reference solution might offer a strong signal. However, when feedback doesn't align structurally with how the model reasons, it spreads corrections throughout the model's sequence of actions, even for correctly followed steps. The step-aligned feedback method directly addresses the model's errors without disrupting the parts where it's already accurate.

But there are limitations. Creating high-quality step-aligned critiques requires robust critique models. This need adds complexity and might increase the cost of training compared to simpler methods. There’s also an open question about whether these findings hold across different datasets and reasoning tasks.

So who should care? If you’re working with large language models, especially for tasks involving complex reasoning, this paper is a must-read. It's time to consider how you design feedback mechanisms and not just the feedback itself. Exploring this could lead to highly efficient and accurate models without needing to source external teacher models.
