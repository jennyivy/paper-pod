The paper titled "Contagion Networks: Evaluator Bias Propagation in Multi-Agent LLM Systems" takes a critical look at how biases in large language model systems, especially when used as evaluators in a network of agents, can spread and potentially skew the output of other agents in the system. This matters because in multi-agent environments, these biases could lead to a uniformity that nullifies the benefits of having diverse strategies at play.

The core contribution of the paper is the introduction of Contagion Networks—a framework to measure and analyze how biases from one agent can propagate to others and influence the entire network. Using a carefully designed experiment, the authors show that biases do indeed flow between agents, but the extent and significance of this flow depend on many factors, including the setup of the evaluators themselves.

Key highlights include the identification of three propagation regimes: suppression, persistence, and cascade, governed by the spectral radius of the contagion matrix. In their experiments using a homogeneous model setup, bias propagation appeared significant but was weaker compared to prior cross-model results. They discovered that in a setup with homogeneous agents, biases diminished rapidly, suggesting that such setups may naturally suppress the propagation of biases. The paper also demonstrates a practical mitigation strategy: increasing the number of evaluators from one to three reduced bias propagation by over seventy percent. Notably, this finding provides a simple yet effective approach to reducing bias in multi-agent systems.

However, the paper does caution that these results are limited to homogeneous model systems and suggest that the dynamics could shift considerably when diverse models are involved.

In summary, if you're involved in building multi-agent systems where LLMs serve as both agents and evaluators, this paper suggests monitoring for potential bias cascades, using the contagion matrix framework, and considering evaluator homogeneity and diversity as key design choices.
